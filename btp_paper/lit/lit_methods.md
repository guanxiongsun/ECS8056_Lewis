## (a) Synthesis

**Where specification curves come from.** The idea that a published result is one draw from many defensible analyses comes from psychology's replication crisis. Undisclosed flexibility "dramatically increases actual false-positive rates" [simmons2011false; E1]. The problem arises "even in settings where researchers perform only a single analysis" [gelman2013garden; E2]. Two constructive answers followed. Multiverse analysis runs every reasonable data-processing choice and shows which ones the conclusion is fragile to [steegen2016increasing; E3]. Specification curve analysis (SCA) adds rules for which specifications are admissible, a sorted curve with a dashboard of the choices behind each estimate, and a joint test against curves resampled under the null [simonsohn2020specification; E4–E15c]. Many-analyst studies show the stakes: 29 teams split 20 to 9 on significance [silberzahn2018many; E16], no two of 70 neuroimaging teams chose the same workflow [botviniknezer2020variability; E17], and over 95% of between-team variance stayed unexplained after coding every decision [breznau2022observing; E18]. A second wave asked what belongs in a multiverse. Auspurg and Brüderl traced the many-analysts divergence mainly to "an unclear research question" [auspurg2021has; E23]. Lundberg et al. ask every study to name its estimand [lundberg2021what; E24]. Del Giudice and Gangestad split decisions into Type E (equivalent, pooled in one multiverse), Type N (non-equivalent, analysed separately) and Type U (uncertain, explored) [delgiudice2021travelers; E19–E22]. This is the template for our split. A pitfall is a convention with a right answer, so its wrong branch fails SCA's "statistically valid" criterion [E4, E5]: it is audited, not varied. A fork that answers a different question is Type N and gets its own curve [E6, E20]. Only Type E choices share a curve. Machine learning has started to borrow the method. There are multiverse analyses of ML claims [bell2022modeling; E30] and of fairness scores, where "decisions regarding the evaluation of a system can lead to vastly different fairness metrics for the same model" [simson2024one; E31]. LLM work names scoring methods [tsvilodub2024predictions; E32] and configuration choices [cummins2025threat; E34] as researcher degrees of freedom. We found no application to robot learning (gap G1).

**What RL and robot-learning evaluation already covers.** Deep RL learned that results hinge on seeds and environment non-determinism [henderson2018deep; E35] and on code-level details, which explain most of PPO's gain over TRPO [engstrom2020implementation; E36; huang2022implementation]. A factorial study crossed more than 50 choices over 250,000 agents [andrychowicz2021what; E37]. Point estimates and interval estimates gave different conclusions [agarwal2021deep; E38; colas2018how; jordan2020evaluating; patterson2024empirical]. Robot learning has absorbed the statistical half of this lesson. Kress-Gazit et al. note that success rates are reported with "little to no information regarding the number of runs, the initial conditions, and the success criteria" [kressgazit2024robot; E42]. They prescribe success criteria, counts per condition, A/B and blind sessions, initial conditions, and interval or Bayesian estimates [E43–E45]. TRI's study runs blind randomised trials with 50 real rollouts per task, policy and condition, and uses compact-letter displays and sequential tests [barreiros2026careful; E46]. Sequential tests avoid "inadvertent p-hacking" [snyder2025policy; E47; snyder2026beyond; E48]. Bounds and prediction-powered inference tighten small-sample claims [vincent2024generalizable; E49; badithela2025reliable; E50]. Proxy evaluators are validated by how well they preserve real-world rankings [kadian2020sim2real; E51; li2024evaluating; E52; atreya2025roboarena; E53; zhou2025autoeval; E54]. The implementation half of the lesson appears in robot learning as "what matters" studies of training, data and action-space choices [mandlekar2021what; E55; feng2026demystifying; E59] and as evaluation-substrate effects [verma2026measuring; E58]. In all of this work the measured quantity is closed-loop success, and the readout that turns a model's output distribution into an action is taken as given. OpenVLA's report that "4-bit quantization matches the performance of bfloat16 inference" is a claim about success on 8 tasks [kim2024openvla; E60]. LLM work shows that exactly this kind of aggregate parity can coexist with many changed individual answers [dutta2024accuracy; E89]. Our probe finds 58% of lateral argmax tokens changed.

**Parallels in LLM evaluation.** LLM evaluation already accepts that the verdict depends on the measurement protocol. Prompt formatting moves accuracy by up to 76 points [sclar2024quantifying; E76]. Single-template results are brittle [mizrahi2024state; E77; polo2024efficient; E80]. The order of few-shot examples [lu2022fantastically; E78] and the answer-selection method [alzahrani2024benchmarks; E81] change results and rankings. Models carry answer priors at the token level [zhao2021calibrate; E79; zheng2024large; E82], the analogue of OpenVLA's rightward lean. The readout is itself a fork. Discontinuous metrics create "emergent" abilities that continuous metrics dissolve [schaeffer2023emergent; E83], which parallels argmax versus expected value. First-token probabilities and generated answers disagree in over 60% of cases [wang2024answer; E84; wang2024look; E85]. Probability scoring has its own aggregation and normalisation problems [holtzman2021surface; E86; hu2023prompting; E87]. Ties make a metric depend on tie-breaking [mcsherry2008computing; E88], and argmax ties occur in 33% of our left/right contrasts. Precision and serving conditions change individual outputs. Compressed models flip answers at equal accuracy [dutta2024accuracy; E89; kurtic2025give; E90], and bf16 greedy decoding drifts with GPU count, type and batch size [yuan2025understanding; E91; he2025defeating; E92; atil2024nondeterminism; E93]. Reproducibility frameworks codify such settings [biderman2024lessons; E94; gu2025olmes; E95; liang2023holistic; E96]. The variance literature asks for score distributions, power and paired or clustered uncertainty [bouthillier2021accounting; E97; dodge2019show; E98; reimers2017reporting; E99; miller2024adding; E100; card2020little; E101]. Equal aggregates can hide different individual predictions [marx2020predictive; E104; damour2022underspecification; E103]. An autoregressive VLA such as OpenVLA inherits this whole decoding stack. It adds layers no LLM paper covers: which action slot is lateral and its sign, the token-to-bin map [E60b, E60c], an autoregressive action prefix, and transformations of the image.

**Probing, shortcuts and paired designs.** Our study is a behavioural probe, and the probing literature gives two cautions. A probe result needs controls that bound what the probe alone can produce [hewitt2019designing; E108; belinkov2022probing; E109; ravichander2021probing; E110]. High scores can come from artifacts or heuristics instead of the intended competence [gururangan2018annotation; E111; mccoy2019right; E112; geirhos2020shortcut; E113]. Paired designs address both: complementary image pairs [goyal2017making; E114], changed priors [agrawal2018dont; E115], contrast sets [gardner2020evaluating; E106], counterfactual data [kaushik2020learning; E107] and twin sentences [elazar2021back; E117]. Winoground's group score credits an item only if every pairing is right, because scoring twins one at a time overestimates performance [thrush2022winoground; E116]. This is the precedent for our decisive statistic: both instructions must send the robot to their own twin. A direction-blind sign-agreement contrast rewards behaviour that is consistent but backwards. Gelman and Carlin call an estimate with the wrong sign a Type S error [gelman2014beyond; E29]. SCA's own "dominant sign" display is deliberately two-sided [E11], so a curve built on direction-blind statistics would repeat this pitfall at scale. Directional statistics must be chosen explicitly (section c, rule 7).

**Reporting standards and the gap they leave.** Documentation standards now exist for models [mitchell2019model; E120], datasets [gebru2021datasheets; E121], language data [bender2018data; E122], ML papers [pineau2021improving; E123; kapoor2024reforms; E124; kapoor2023leakage; E125] and benchmarks [reuel2024betterbench; E128; sokol2025benchmarkcards; E129; bean2025measuring; E130]. Only 16.0% of 445 LLM benchmarks used uncertainty estimates or statistical tests [E130]. Eval Factsheets observes that "evaluation methodologies lack systematic documentation standards" [bordes2025eval; E73]. Measurement theory gives the vocabulary [jacobs2021measurement; E119; wallach2025position; E131]: the action readout operationalises a construct ("grounding"), and verdicts flip where the operationalisation departs from the construct. Data statements ask for the "speech situation" and "text characteristics" of language data [bender2018data; E122]. For robot datasets, this is where the conventions for spatial words would be recorded; in BridgeData V2, spatial words mostly name destinations. Vision-language work already traces left/right failures to training language [kamath2023whats; E118]. In robotics, reporting guidance covers rollout conditions [kressgazit2024robot], evidence tiers and fixed inference configurations [wang2026industrialvla; E63], shared action schemas [xpolicylab2026xpolicylab; E72] and, in grey literature, sampling settings, action normalisation, paraphrases and minimal pairs [physicalailab2026robot; E74]. The workshop's own checklist v0.1, to be produced after the workshop, will cover "hardware and embodiment ... action spaces, observations, demonstrations, simulation, reward design, and reset distributions" [park2026everything; E75]. None of these has items for the evaluation readout layer: readout statistic and ties, action slot and sign, bin map, precision, prefix, transformed-image scoring, stimulus screening, decisive statistic, and wording as estimand. Our reporting card fills that layer, and our audit shows that each item can change a verdict.

## (c) Specification-curve rules we will follow

Source: Simonsohn, Simmons & Nelson (2020), *Nature Human Behaviour* 4:1208–1214, doi:10.1038/s41562-020-0912-z. The full text was read from the authors' copy of the published article (https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/33-Simonsohn-Simmons-Nelson-2020.pdf) and the abstract was checked on nature.com. The tags refer to the quotes in (b2). The Type E/N/U layer comes from Del Giudice & Gangestad (2021).

**What enters a curve**

1. **Admissible specifications.** Include every "reasonable specification": (1) a sensible test of the research question, (2) expected to be statistically valid, and (3) not redundant with another specification in the set [E4]. A specification that "does not make sense theoretically or statistically", or is "unambiguously inferior", does not belong in a specification curve [E5]. Two analyses that answer different questions are not a robustness check, even if both are interesting [E6].
2. **Equivalence layer (Del Giudice & Gangestad).** Only Type E (equivalent) alternatives populate one curve [E19, E19b]. Type N (non-equivalent) alternatives go into separate analyses [E20]. Type U (uncertain) alternatives are explored deliberately [E21]. Mixing non-arbitrary alternatives can hide or fake effects [E22].
3. **Building the set.** (i) List every decision needed to map the hypothesis onto a statistical test. (ii) List the reasonable alternatives for each decision. (iii) Take all combinations and drop the invalid or redundant ones. If the set is too large, sample it at random [E7]. Redundant alternatives are near-duplicates, for example log(x+1) versus log(x+1.1), and only one of them is kept. An expert, not an algorithm, defines the set [E14].

**How the curve is shown and tested**

4. **Descriptive curve.** Plot the estimate from every specification, sorted by size, with a "dashboard" underneath marking the choices behind each point. This shows which decisions matter.
5. **Joint test statistics** [E8]:
   - (a) the median effect across specifications;
   - (b) the share of specifications that are significant in the predicted direction;
   - (c) Stouffer's Z, the per-specification Z values averaged with weight 1/√K. Its p-value is also obtained by resampling, because specifications are dependent [E15].

   Report both (b), which is intuitive, and (c), which is more efficient [E15b].
6. **Null distribution and p-value.** For experimental data, shuffle the randomly assigned variable and re-estimate *all* specifications on each shuffled dataset. The only assumption is exchangeability [E9]. For observational data, force the null instead: compute y* = y − b̂x for each specification, then bootstrap rows, using the same drawn rows for all K specifications [E12]. The paper uses 500 resamples. The p-value is the share of null curves at least as extreme as the observed one, with exact ties counted as one half [E10]. With 500 resamples and no exceedance, report p < 0.002.
7. **Direction.** The paper's sign display uses the "dominant sign" and is two-sided [E11]. For a directional claim ("left" should move toward the left twin), use the directional statistics in rule 5: (b) as written, and (a) and (c) with signs kept. Do not use dominant-sign or direction-blind statistics. These would score consistently backwards behaviour as an effect, which is our decisive-statistic pitfall.
8. **Known limitations.** By default every specification gets equal weight [E15c]; weighted variants of all three statistics are possible. A whole curve can be pre-registered [E13]. The joint test shows that the curve as a whole departs from the null, but not which specifications are significant [E26]. PIMA [girardi2024post] adds family-wise error control using sign-flip score tests.

**Suggested mapping onto our probe** (a suggestion for the main author; it should follow the analysis actually run)

- **Sampling units and null.** The scene is the unit, and scenes from the same base frame form a cluster [miller2024adding]. Within a scene, the instruction word (left or right) is assigned by design, so randomly flipping the word label within each scene generates the null of no word effect. This is SCA's shuffle for experiments, and equivalent to a sign-flip test. Each permuted dataset is pushed through every specification in the curve.
- **Pitfalls: excluded from every curve, shown in a separate audit** that reports how the verdict changes under the wrong convention:
  - which action slot is lateral, and its sign;
  - the token-to-bin map;
  - the scoring of mirrored images (flip the image, swap the word, negate the lateral action);
  - direction-blind sign agreement as the decisive statistic.
- **Forks that change the estimand (Type N): separate curves**, each with its own joint test:
  - argmax (modal bin) versus expected value (mean action);
  - the model's own decoded prefix versus a fixed prefix;
  - instruction templates whose word effect differs in sign, which answer different questions;
  - possibly the bf16 reference versus the deployed 4-bit model.
- **Type E choices populate a single curve**, for example bin-width or aggregation variants within the expected-value readout, and screening variants judged equivalent.
- **Type U choices go into exploratory panels**, for example a screening rule whose approval rates differ by condition [E21].
- **Report** K, the dashboard, the three statistics with permutation p-values, and the share of specifications whose sign agrees with the headline claim.

**Strongest defensible statement of novelty**

> To our knowledge, this is the first specification-curve analysis of a robot-learning evaluation. It follows Simonsohn et al.'s admissibility rules and resampling-based joint inference, and it is the first to aim such an analysis at the *measurement layer* of a VLA language probe rather than at training, data or environment choices. Robot-learning methodology now offers rigorous statistics for closed-loop success: trial counts, blinding, interval and sequential tests, and sim-to-real rank agreement. But it treats the readout of the policy's output as fixed. VLA robustness work varies wording only as a perturbation of a fixed success metric. We show that nine choices at the readout layer each changed a result in one model-organism study: the lateral action slot and its sign, the token-to-bin map, argmax versus expected value, numerical precision, the autoregressive prefix, the scoring of mirrored images, stimulus screening, the decisive statistic, and wording. Using Del Giudice and Gangestad's Type E/N/U distinction, we separate the conventions that have a right answer (pitfalls, audited) from the choices that define different estimands (forks, separate curves). We turn both into reporting items that existing standards lack: model cards, datasheets, data statements, Eval Factsheets and the announced scope of the BtP checklist. The LLM parallels are well established: prompt-format sensitivity, first-token versus generated readouts, flips under quantisation, numerical nondeterminism. What is new is their transfer to action-token VLAs, together with robot-specific conventions that have no LLM counterpart: action slots and signs, bin maps, and mirrored scenes.

What we can *not* claim: that VLA evaluations have never been sensitive to wording or precision (they have, at the success level: G3, G4); that no one has used VLA action-token probabilities (zollo2025confidence); or that robot learning lacks statistical guidance (G2).
